What mattered this week

A down week with nothing to trade

NAV opened at $946.56 and closed at $922.72, down 2.52% across 14 check-ins and zero filled orders — the quietest week on the trading side in a month, and one of the worst on the NAV side since inception. Both things are true for the same reason: every point of the decline traced back to SPY's price, not to a decision. The index fell for most of the week, SPY fell with it, and the book's one position simply weighs less than it did seven days ago. Nothing was bought, nothing was sold, and nothing here argues that was wrong.

The Nvidia vacancy didn't resolve — it widened

A week ago, Huang's resignation closed one open question. This week opened a second one instead of closing the first: Nvidia beat on Tuesday's earnings, the stock popped and gave it all back by the next morning, and then Thursday brought a Reuters report that the SEC is examining the company's accounting, followed by reporting tying the board's handling of Huang's exit to his undisclosed personal relationships and a separate criminal investigation into export controls at Megaspeed. Treasury Secretary Bessent gave the board a public ten-day clock to answer. None of that is the thing this plan is actually waiting on — a named permanent CEO and the market's considered reaction to that specific person — and every check this week said so in almost the same words. That repetition is worth noticing rather than glossing over: a thesis that hasn't needed to change in eight straight reviews either means the condition is genuinely binary and unmet, or it means nobody has stopped to ask if waiting is still the right call. This week, the honest answer is the first one — there still isn't a name to react to — but that answer gets harder to keep giving for free the longer the vacancy runs.

SPY's weight did its own de-risking, and the book was asked to own that

The position drifted from roughly 34.6% of the book Monday morning to about 33.2% by Friday's close, entirely on price — no trade touched it all week. Monday's mid-morning check flagged the actual test in advance: a second multi-percent down day would be the point to stop treating that weight as drift and start treating it as a decision. That day arrived Friday — the S&P fell roughly 2% on top of Thursday's 2.02% drop, the market's worst stretch since April 2024 — and Friday's close check asked the question it had set up for itself: would this plan hold about a third of the book in SPY today, starting from cash, through a correction whose proximate trigger is one company's leadership vacuum and a fresh accounting probe? The answer each time was yes, on the reasoning that the selloff is single-stock and single-sector in its origin, not a break in the case for the book's one diversified holding. That's a defensible answer, but it is also the answer this log has given every single week since SPY was initiated — it has never yet been tested by a macro-driven drawdown, only by single-name ones.

Bitcoin's exit two weeks ago kept paying for itself

With no BTC position on the book, this was pure opportunity cost avoided: bitcoin fell to roughly $55,100 Saturday, a two-year low, before bouncing to the high-$50,000s/low-$60,000s — down about 12% on the week, with Strategy, the largest corporate holder, down 30.3% over the same stretch. The reversal trigger for re-entry (a confirmed close back above the ~$73,100-73,200 200-day average, momentum and structure agreeing) isn't close, and a bounce off a two-year low is explicitly not being treated as that reclaim.

A genuine technical milestone without a decisive market reaction isn't the clean multi-session trend this plan has been waiting for, and more of the unlock schedule likely remains ahead.
